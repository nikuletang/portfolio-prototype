01 Project overview

Most reports arrived by phone, not through the web form

The problem

DC Water has a web form for reporting problems, but most reports come in through the emergency hotline. That leads to a high volume of calls and slower response times.

DC Water distributes drinking water and collects and treats wastewater for residents and visitors of the District of Columbia. Its web form lets people report issues such as leaks and broken fire hydrants, so customers and DC Water can resolve problems in homes and neighborhoods together.

Design challenge

DC Water wants to drive users to its mobile-responsive web form and make reporting problems more efficient.

03 Understanding the problem

Learning how people describe water problems

The form used DC Water's terminology. To fix that, we first needed to understand how people themselves group and name the problems they see.

Starting point · Findings from Sprints 1–2

What earlier testing revealed

User testing in Sprints 1 and 2, before I joined, surfaced three problems with the existing design:

  1. Terminology was too technical

    Users were unsure how to define the issue they wanted to report.

  2. "Emergency" made people hesitate

    Users hesitated to use the Emergency form because they were not confident their issue was a "true emergency."

  3. Some issues were missing

    Other possible issues were not represented in the design, and users had no way to select them.

Sprint 3 goals

Improve the current design to…

  • Help users build confidence in making selections in the form
  • Clearly communicate the criteria for real emergencies
  • Provide more possible combinations of issue types and affected equipment
  • Revise terminology to match users' mental model
Research · Sprint 3

Image card sorting

Question: Because users had difficulty matching the issues on the form with the issue they wanted to report, we ran an unmoderated card sort to learn how they define and categorize issues.

  • We pulled images from past submissions reported through the "Report a Problem" form and Twitter.
  • Participants grouped similar images together and gave each group a name.

Research · Sprint 3

Word bank and image association

Question: Which words do people connect with each kind of problem?

Once we knew how users categorized issues, we turned their category names into a word bank. We then asked participants to think aloud about what they saw in each image and match as many words as they liked to it.

Key findings from card sorting and image association

  • Categorization varied widely. "Flooding" and "fire hydrant" were the only categories users had in common. Flooding / Leaking appears as a problem type in the information architecture and in every later iteration.
  • Participants associated issues with their setting: indoors or outdoors. The top level of the information architecture is Indoor, Outdoor, and Not Sure / Other.
  • Users misidentified more technical objects, such as a manhole and a water meter. Echoed in Sprint 5: participants valued object descriptions that taught them the terminology while reporting.
Synthesis · Sprint 3

The resulting information architecture

We used the card sorting results as the basis for the information architecture. Problems are first grouped by where they happen, then by a small set of plain-language problem types, with an escape hatch for people who aren't sure.

Usability testing · Sprint 3

What testing the first iteration showed

Wins

  • The form met user expectations and was understandable and easy to use.
  • Most participants added their contact information and agreed to receive alerts.
  • Users appreciated being able to add tags to give their issue more context.
  • Participants found the ticket tracking process helpful.
  • Users appreciated being able to review and edit their information before submitting.

4.6 / 5 average confidence score

Opportunities

  • "Emergency" language may cause hesitancy to fill out the form.
  • Ticket status: users wanted to be reminded of what they submitted.
  • Data privacy: some worried their contact information or data could be used to send unrelated alerts later.
  • Users were initially unsure how to categorize a problem that could fit several categories.
  • There was no option to choose a notification preference.
  • Users wanted to add a comment after submitting the form.
  • Most users couldn't think of a scenario where they'd use "Other" for a problem that is neither indoors nor outdoors.
Iteration 2 · Sprint 4

Design decisions in the second iteration

In Sprint 4 we updated the web form based on the testing results. Each change below traces from a finding to the redesign.

Terminology: from "Report an Emergency" to "Report a Problem"

  1. What we learned"Emergency" language may make people hesitate, and earlier testing showed users weren't sure their issue was a true emergency.
  2. What changedThe form title changed from "Report an Emergency" to "Report a Problem," to make users less likely to abandon the form when they aren't sure their issue is a true emergency.
  3. What testing showedNo follow-up test of this change is documented.

Notifications: preferences and clearer consent

  1. What we learnedThere was no way to choose a notification preference, and some participants worried their data could be used for unrelated alerts.
  2. What changedA simpler, more concise contact form with notification preferences. Receiving updates was separated from allowing DC Water to make contact, and the screen states that DC Water will only contact the user about this ticket.
  3. What testing showedNo follow-up test of this change is documented.

Ticket tracking: show what was submitted

  1. What we learnedParticipants found ticket tracking helpful but wanted to be reminded of what they had submitted.
  2. What changedThe ticket status page was redesigned to show the ticket's status and a new "Ticket Details" tab with the submitted information. An option to rate the experience and leave feedback was also added.
  3. What testing showedNo follow-up test of this change is documented.

Categories: fewer choices up front, a clear route when unsure

  1. What we learnedUsers were unsure how to categorize problems that fit several categories, and most couldn't imagine using "Other" for a problem that was neither indoors nor outdoors.
  2. What the final screens show"Where is the problem?" offers only Outdoors and Indoors, with a link to describe the issue if unsure. "Other / Not Sure" moves to the problem-type step. The final flow also starts with a location step.
  3. What testing showedNo follow-up test of this change is documented.
Customer Service form · Sprint 4

Separating problems from inquiries

DC Water's previous form combined emergency issues and miscellaneous inquiries in one form, which confused users. We decided to direct people to two different forms based on their issue or inquiry, and designed a new Customer Service form for miscellaneous issues such as billing inquiries.

First, we worked with DC Water to decide which options could be left out of the new form because they were covered elsewhere on its website.

05 Exploring photo-based reporting

Could a photo do the describing for you?

We finished the Report a Problem and Customer Service forms earlier than expected, so in Sprint 5 we designed alternative ways to report a problem.

ConceptImage recognition is a concept, designed and tested as a Figma prototype. The recognition shown is simulated, not working functionality.

Exploration brief · Sprint 5

The brief

Sprint 4's plan included designing an artificial intelligence application to give users an alternative, more efficient way to report a problem. Sprint 5 set out to:

  1. Research existing products that use machine learning and artificial intelligence to see whether there's an opportunity for a new Report a Problem solution.

  2. Run lightning demos on alternative products.

  3. Sketch new ideas.

  4. Design new products.

Lightning demos

Purpose: find inspiring products, services, or experiences that could be adapted to DC Water's need for reporting water-related issues.

Standout examples included, but weren't limited to:

  • WazeReal-time traffic reporting
  • ChipotleChatbot
  • Google Lens 
  • AppleText support

Sketching and alternatives considered

The lightning demos triggered a wide range of ideas. The team brought them to DC Water, who voted for the ones they believed would meet users' needs while being logistically and financially feasible.

  • Public kioskNot pursued
  • Image recognitionSelected by DC Water · my focus
  • ChatbotSelected by DC Water
  • SMS solutionNot pursued
  • Video conferencingNot pursued

The team divided the work. I worked only on image recognition, so it's the only concept in this case study.

Comparative testing · Sprint 5

Testing two directions for the landing and details screens

After the first iteration, we had different ideas for the interface and interactions, especially for the landing page and the problem details page. The team couldn't agree on the best options, so we tested both versions of each pair of screens with users.

  • 6 participants, all of whom had tested the Report a Problem form
  • Goals: overall impressions of image recognition, and how it compares with the Report a Problem form for ease of use and effectiveness

Landing page

Problem details

Wins

  • The photo layout landing page was simple and easy to use.
  • Users noticed and used the additional photo button on the full-page problem details layout.
  • The design met users' expectations about what would happen after uploading a photo.
  • Users appreciated the object descriptions after uploading a photo because they could learn the terminology while reporting.
  • Users liked that tags were selected automatically after uploading a photo, and appreciated being able to select more.

6 of 6 participants said they'd prefer image recognition over the Report a Problem form (prototype preference)

Opportunities

  • The "Image Identifier" label on the split-button landing page confused some users, who didn't know what to expect.
  • Users were unlikely to close the drawer on the problem details page unless they wanted a full view of their image.
  • Users didn't notice the additional photo button in the drawer layout.
  • Users found the confirmation message after selecting "Submit" unnecessary.
  • Users didn't know how to save the ticket number after submitting.
Iteration 2 · Sprint 5

Revisions after testing

Submitting and tracking without a ticket number

  1. What we learnedThe confirmation message after "Submit" felt unnecessary, and users didn't know how to save the ticket number.
  2. What changedThe extra submit step was removed: users get a thank-you message and can exit right away. The copy-ticket-number option was removed, and users track their report by uploading the original photo.
  3. What testing showedNo test of the second iteration is documented. Tracking by photo introduced a new limitation (see Outcomes).

Landing page and problem details layouts

  1. What we learnedThe photo layout landing page was simple and easy to use, while "Image Identifier" confused some users. Users noticed the additional photo button on the full-page layout but not in the drawer.
  2. What the final screens showThe final prototype uses the photo layout landing page ("Show Us The Issue") and the full-page problem details, where users can add another photo.
  3. What testing showedNo test of the second iteration is documented.

07 Outcomes, limitations, and reflection

What was designed, tested, delivered, and left as a recommendation

Designed

  • Report a Problem form: research-based IA and two iterations
  • New Customer Service form
  • Image recognition concept: two iterations

Tested (prototypes)

  • Report a Problem iteration 1: usability testing, average confidence 4.6 / 5
  • Image recognition iteration 1: A/B testing with 6 participants; 6 of 6 preferred it over the form
  • Participants came from the team's personal network

Delivered

  • Sprint 4 goals included handing deliverables off to developers and collaborating with them regularly
  • Project status: development in progress

Concept / recommendation

  • Image recognition (not implemented)
  • Explore a public kiosk
  • Consider an app for customers with online accounts

Limitations and future recommendations

Constraints that shaped the research and design, and where I'd take the work next.

  1. Research participants weren't DC Water's target users

    Anyone can use the Report a Problem form, but we didn't have access to target users who live in DC and, ideally, have reported a problem to DC Water before, and the client couldn't provide them. The participants in user testing and research came from the team's personal network. We did, however, have access to previously submitted reports, which we used to create user scenarios and the content for the card sorting activity.

  2. A responsive web form instead of an app

    After consulting with the client, the team designed a responsive web form instead of a mobile app, assuming that most people who come across an outdoor issue once won't be invested enough to download an app just to report it. However, this design doesn't address DC Water customers with online accounts, who might be more invested and more willing to use an app for easy access.

  3. Tracking depends on the original photo

    The image recognition design needs the original photo to track a report. If the user loses the photo, they have no way to track their report.

  4. People without mobile devices

    Accessibility and inclusion are very important to me, and I try to build them into my work. When considering who the product might exclude, I thought about people experiencing homelessness. They may encounter outdoor issues that affect them but likely don't have mobile devices to report a problem. I think a public kiosk should be explored as an alternative because it isn't individually owned.

Reflection

Seeing the design become a usable product is rewarding

This project challenged me intellectually and creatively and pushed me to use my analytical skills in every sprint. Before it, I had very little experience working with an external client and translating their business needs into a usable product that also met users' expectations.

The most rewarding part is that, with the help of developers, our designs will become a live product on DC Water's website for anyone who lives in or visits the District of Columbia. I enjoyed learning strategies for a successful hand-off to developers, but I wish we'd explored this area more. I would have liked more engagement with the client team across iterations, and in particular, it would have helped to know the financial possibilities and constraints while designing.

Being adaptable and flexible is a must

Because I joined after Sprint 2 ended, I wasn't part of the client kick-off meeting or the expert interviews. Information from those meetings would have given me a deeper understanding of the business need and problem area.

But this is how work happens outside the classroom: UX teams must be adaptable and flexible as client and user needs change and new trends emerge. I'm grateful to the team members from earlier sprints for providing context and bringing me up to speed.

Teamwork wins

I learned to collaborate more effectively with a team. In my professional career I've mostly been responsible for coming up with creative, technology-based solutions. Working with this team challenged me to be open-minded and sometimes consider the big picture instead of every detail.

The team's size was challenging at times: there were many different opinions, sometimes conflicting, which left us short on time. I'm very appreciative of everyone's diverse experiences, skills, and strengths, and proud of the work we all contributed to deliver a great product to our client.
