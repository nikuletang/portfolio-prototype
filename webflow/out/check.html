01Project overview

Most reports arrived by phone, not through the web form

The problem

DC Water has a web form for reporting problems, but most reports come in through the emergency hotline. That leads to a high volume of calls and slower response times.

DC Water distributes drinking water and collects and treats wastewater for residents and visitors of the District of Columbia. Its web form lets people report issues such as leaks and broken fire hydrants, so customers and DC Water can resolve problems in homes and neighborhoods together.

Design challenge

DC Water wants to drive users to its mobile-responsive web form and make reporting problems more efficient.

03Understanding the problem

Learning how people describe water problems

The form used DC Water's terminology. To fix that, we first needed to understand how people themselves group and name the problems they see.

Starting point · Findings from Sprints 1–2

What earlier testing revealed

User testing in Sprints 1 and 2, before I joined, surfaced three problems with the existing design:

01

Terminology was too technical

Users were unsure how to define the issue they wanted to report.

02

“Emergency” made people hesitate

Users hesitated to use the Emergency form because they were not confident their issue was a “true emergency.”

03

Some issues were missing

Other possible issues were not represented in the design, and users had no way to select them.

Sprint 3 goals

Improve the current design to…

01
Help users build confidence in making selections in the form
02
Clearly communicate the criteria for real emergencies
03
Provide more possible combinations of issue types and affected equipment
04
Revise terminology to match users' mental model

Usability testing · Sprint 3

What testing the first iteration showed

Wins
  • The form met user expectations and was understandable and easy to use.
  • Most participants added their contact information and agreed to receive alerts.
  • Users appreciated being able to add tags to give their issue more context.
  • Participants found the ticket tracking process helpful.
  • Users appreciated being able to review and edit their information before submitting.
4.6 / 5average confidence score
Opportunities
  • “Emergency” language may cause hesitancy to fill out the form.
  • Ticket status: users wanted to be reminded of what they submitted.
  • Data privacy: some worried their contact information or data could be used to send unrelated alerts later.
  • Users were initially unsure how to categorize a problem that could fit several categories.
  • There was no option to choose a notification preference.
  • Users wanted to add a comment after submitting the form.
  • Most users couldn't think of a scenario where they'd use “Other” for a problem that is neither indoors nor outdoors.

Comparative testing · Sprint 5

Testing two directions for the landing and details screens

After the first iteration, we had different ideas for the interface and interactions, especially for the landing page and the problem details page. The team couldn't agree on the best options, so we tested both versions of each pair of screens with users.

6 participants, all of whom had tested the Report a Problem form. Goals: overall impressions of image recognition, and how it compares with the Report a Problem form for ease of use and effectiveness.

Landing page

Option A
Split button

Like the Report a Problem landing page, but “Report a Problem” splits into two buttons: fill out the traditional form or use image recognition.

Option B
Photo layout

Image recognition is the default. “Show us the issue” then offers image recognition or the traditional form. This assumed image recognition would be the easiest, most efficient option and that users would want to start with the fastest one.

Problem details

Option A
Interaction drawer

A drawer pattern we found in existing image recognition products. We liked its modern look but worried drawers are native to apps and might not translate well to responsive web pages.

Option B
Full page

A more classic layout, which we liked because the “Add Photo” button is very visible.

Wins
  • The photo layout landing page was simple and easy to use.
  • Users noticed and used the additional photo button on the full-page problem details layout.
  • The design met users' expectations about what would happen after uploading a photo.
  • Users appreciated the object descriptions after uploading a photo because they could learn the terminology while reporting.
  • Users liked that tags were selected automatically after uploading a photo, and appreciated being able to select more.
6 of 6participants said they'd prefer image recognition over the Report a Problem form (prototype preference)
Opportunities
  • The “Image Identifier” label on the split-button landing page confused some users, who didn't know what to expect.
  • Users were unlikely to close the drawer on the problem details page unless they wanted a full view of their image.
  • Users didn't notice the additional photo button in the drawer layout.
  • Users found the confirmation message after selecting “Submit” unnecessary.
  • Users didn't know how to save the ticket number after submitting.

Limitations and future recommendations

Constraints that shaped the research and design, and where I'd take the work next.

01

Research participants weren't DC Water's target users

Anyone can use the Report a Problem form, but we didn't have access to target users who live in DC and, ideally, have reported a problem to DC Water before, and the client couldn't provide them. The participants in user testing and research came from the team's personal network. We did, however, have access to previously submitted reports, which we used to create user scenarios and the content for the card sorting activity.

02

A responsive web form instead of an app

After consulting with the client, the team designed a responsive web form instead of a mobile app, assuming that most people who come across an outdoor issue once won't be invested enough to download an app just to report it. However, this design doesn't address DC Water customers with online accounts, who might be more invested and more willing to use an app for easy access.

03

Tracking depends on the original photo

The image recognition design needs the original photo to track a report. If the user loses the photo, they have no way to track their report.

04

People without mobile devices

Accessibility and inclusion are very important to me, and I try to build them into my work. When considering who the product might exclude, I thought about people experiencing homelessness. They may encounter outdoor issues that affect them but likely don't have mobile devices to report a problem. I think a public kiosk should be explored as an alternative because it isn't individually owned.

Reflection

Three lessons from the project, one per page. Use the arrows, the page tabs, or your keyboard's arrow keys to turn the page.

Reflection 01

Seeing the design become a usable product is rewarding

This project challenged me intellectually and creatively and pushed me to use my analytical skills in every sprint. Before it, I had very little experience working with an external client and translating their business needs into a usable product that also met users' expectations.

The most rewarding part is that, with the help of developers, our designs will become a live product on DC Water's website for anyone who lives in or visits the District of Columbia. I enjoyed learning strategies for a successful hand-off to developers, but I wish we'd explored this area more. I would have liked more engagement with the client team across iterations, and in particular, it would have helped to know the financial possibilities and constraints while designing.

1 / 3
Reflection 02

Being adaptable and flexible is a must

Because I joined after Sprint 2 ended, I wasn't part of the client kick-off meeting or the expert interviews. Information from those meetings would have given me a deeper understanding of the business need and problem area.

But this is how work happens outside the classroom: UX teams must be adaptable and flexible as client and user needs change and new trends emerge. I'm grateful to the team members from earlier sprints for providing context and bringing me up to speed.

2 / 3
Reflection 03

Teamwork wins

I learned to collaborate more effectively with a team. In my professional career I've mostly been responsible for coming up with creative, technology-based solutions. Working with this team challenged me to be open-minded and sometimes consider the big picture instead of every detail.

The team's size was challenging at times: there were many different opinions, sometimes conflicting, which left us short on time. I'm very appreciative of everyone's diverse experiences, skills, and strengths, and proud of the work we all contributed to deliver a great product to our client.

3 / 3
